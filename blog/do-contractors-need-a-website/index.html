Do Contractors Need a Website to Get More Leads?

May 16, 2026 6 min read Studio-Serra

The short answer is yes. If you are a contractor working in Los Angeles or Orange County, a website is not optional — it is the single most reliable tool for generating leads from people who are actively looking to hire. Here is why, and what your site actually needs to do the job.

Why referrals alone aren't enough

Referrals are great. Most contractors in LA built their business on word-of-mouth, and there is nothing wrong with that. But referrals have a ceiling. You cannot scale them, you cannot predict them, and when a dry spell hits, there is nothing you can turn on.

A website works the other direction. Someone in Burbank searches "roofer near me" at 10pm on a Tuesday. They are ready to hire. They click the first few results, look at the websites, and call whoever looks most trustworthy. If you are not in those results, you never existed to that customer.

The real cost of no website

The average homeowner project in Los Angeles is worth $3,000 to $15,000. If your website brings in even one additional job per month, it pays for itself ten times over. The cost of not having one is every job that found your competitor on Google instead of you.

What homeowners do before they call

Before a homeowner in Los Angeles calls any contractor, they typically do three things:

  • Search Google for the service and city ("electrician Pasadena", "plumber Torrance")
  • Check the websites of the top results to see if they look legitimate
  • Look for photos, services listed, and a phone number they can call or text

If you have no website, or a website that looks outdated or unprofessional, most homeowners will move on without contacting you. First impressions are made in seconds online, and there is no second chance once someone clicks away.

What a contractor website needs to actually work

Not all websites generate leads. A website that is just a digital business card does not do much. A website that is built to rank on Google and convert visitors into calls is a different tool entirely. Here is what the latter needs:

  • Services listed clearly — what you do, in plain language, for someone scanning on a phone
  • Service area specified — which cities and neighborhoods you serve (this is critical for local SEO)
  • Photos of real work — before and after photos, completed projects, your team on the job
  • A phone number visible at the top — do not make someone scroll to find how to contact you
  • A way to request a quote — a form or a direct link to text/call
  • Mobile-optimized layout — most searches happen on a phone; your site needs to load and look right on mobile

Local SEO is what separates a website that ranks from one that doesn't

You can have a perfectly designed website that nobody ever sees. Local SEO is the technical setup that tells Google what you do, where you do it, and why you should rank above the next contractor. It includes things like page titles, meta descriptions, your Google Business Profile, and how your service areas are written into the page content.

Most DIY websites and cheap freelancer builds skip this entirely. The result is a website that exists but does not rank, which means it does not generate leads.

For contractors in Los Angeles, getting local SEO right from the start is the difference between a site that pays for itself and one that just sits there.

What about Yelp, Angie's List, or HomeAdvisor?

These platforms have their place. Yelp reviews help with trust. Lead-gen platforms like Angie's List can bring in jobs. But they come with real downsides:

  • You pay per lead — often $20 to $80 per lead whether you win the job or not
  • You are competing on price against every other contractor on the platform
  • The platform owns the customer relationship, not you
  • If the platform changes its pricing or algorithm, your lead flow changes overnight

A website is an asset you own. Once it ranks, leads come in without a per-lead fee. You control the messaging and the customer experience from the first click.
